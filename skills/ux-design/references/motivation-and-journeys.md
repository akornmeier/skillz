# Motivation and journeys

Use these principles for sustained engagement, visible progress, interruption, and remembered experiences. Motivation patterns must support a user-chosen goal—not pressure people into continued use, spending, disclosure, or subscription.

## Flow

**Principle.** Deep engagement is more likely when challenge and skill are reasonably matched, goals are clear, feedback is timely, and avoidable interruption is low.

**Use when:** creative tools, learning, games, editors, or repeated expert workflows feel either frustrating or tedious.

**Design moves:** provide clear next actions and feedback; let novices ramp up while experts use shortcuts; preserve focus and state; remove unnecessary modal interruptions; tune challenge from observed ability rather than assumptions.

**Failure modes:** equating compulsion with flow; hiding breaks or limits; eliminating useful reflection; adding gamification unrelated to the user’s goal.

**Validate:** task continuity, voluntary return, error recovery, challenge ratings, and novice/expert performance. Ask whether engagement serves the user.

Source: https://lawsofux.com/flow/

## Goal-Gradient Effect

**Principle.** Motivation can increase as a person perceives that a valued goal is getting closer.

**Use when:** setup, onboarding, applications, learning paths, profile completion, or multi-step tasks suffer late-stage abandonment.

**Design moves:** show truthful progress, completed work, remaining effort, and a meaningful endpoint; make the next step concrete; save progress and allow resumption.

**Failure modes:** fake head starts, progress that moves backward without explanation, hiding steps until late, encouraging completion of low-value profile data, or using completion pressure as a dark pattern.

**Validate:** step-level abandonment, completion, return after interruption, progress comprehension, and whether users consider the goal worthwhile.

Source: https://lawsofux.com/goal-gradient-effect/

## Peak-End Rule

**Principle.** Memory of an experience is often shaped disproportionately by its most intense moments and ending, rather than by a simple average of every moment.

**Use when:** checkout, publishing, recovery from failure, support, cancellation, achievement, and confirmation moments shape overall perception.

**Design moves:** identify emotional peaks; reduce anxiety and uncertainty there; make endings clear, respectful, and useful; confirm outcomes and next steps; invest especially in recovery after serious errors.

**Failure modes:** polishing a celebratory ending while leaving the journey harmful; forcing delight or animation; neglecting negative peaks; making cancellation endings punitive.

**Validate:** journey interviews, event-specific satisfaction, delayed recall, support contacts, and behavior after successful or failed completion.

Source: https://lawsofux.com/peak-end-rule/

## Zeigarnik Effect

**Principle.** Incomplete or interrupted tasks can remain cognitively salient, which may aid resumption but can also create stress.

**Use when:** users need to resume drafts, discover remaining steps, or maintain continuity across sessions.

**Design moves:** save state; show what remains; provide clear resumption cues; allow dismissal, snoozing, or intentional abandonment; distinguish required from optional completion.

**Failure modes:** persistent nagging, notification pressure, ambiguous “almost done” messages, infinite completion loops, or exploiting unfinished-task tension for retention.

**Validate:** resumption success, time to regain context, opt-out behavior, notification burden, and reported stress—not just return rate.

Source: https://lawsofux.com/zeigarnik-effect/

## Combined checks

- Is the goal chosen and valued by the user?
- Is progress truthful, stable, and based on known work?
- Can users pause, resume, or abandon without penalty or shame?
- Are the most stressful and final moments designed for clarity and recovery?
- Does engagement support task success rather than business-driven compulsion?
